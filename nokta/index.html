How it works.

Nokta is a handheld with seven classic games, a real D-pad and a battery that lasts an afternoon. It is a rebuild of the open-source Cihaz console for people who want one that closes properly: soldered wires instead of a nest of jumper leads, a charger inside, and firmware whose pin numbers are generated from the wiring. Every game is one small file, so the next game is yours to write.

1

Slide it on

The switch under the screen starts it straight into the game menu, with your points and volume where you left them.

2

Pick a game

D-pad to choose, A to play, Home to come back. Snake, Pong, Tetris, Breakout, Invaders, PacBoy and FlapBoy are on board.

3

Charge, then change it

Charge through the port on the left side while you keep playing. Plug the top port into a computer to flash a game of your own.

The same seven games. A console that closes.

A D-pad, not a thumbstick

Every game here moves in four directions, so four switches under printed caps replace the analog stick: nothing to calibrate, nothing to drift. A and B are 10 mm across, where your right thumb expects them.

Plays while it charges

The charger board shares power between the battery and the console, and the power switch only carries a signal, never the battery current.

No pin numbers in the code

The firmware's pin map is written from the same file as the build guide, so the wiring and the code cannot disagree.

Sound that does not stall the game

Tones play from a queue in the background. In the original, every beep froze the game until it had finished.

Checked before it is built

Each connection passes an electrical rule check: voltage levels, the load on the 5 V supply, grounds, and controller pins with a catch.

One wire per step

37 soldered wires, each its own numbered drawing with a close-up of both pads, in a case cut from the parts' dimensions.

From every side.

Rendered and drawn from the same files the case is printed from.

What's inside.

16 parts. Total: about 65 USD, estimated from typical prices, not sourced.

Charger and 5 V supply

Charges a single LiPo cell from micro-USB, protects it, and boosts it to a steady 5 V for the rest of the build; it keeps running the build while it charges, and a small switch on its EN pin turns everything off.
1 xabout 20 USD, estimated

Battery

A flat rechargeable 3.7 V lithium-polymer battery with its own protection board and a plug that clicks into the charger.
1 xabout 14 USD, estimated

Power switch

A small two-position slide switch; use it as the power switch between a battery and the rest of the build.
1 xabout 0.50 USD, estimated

Screen

A 2.8 inch colour screen, 240 by 320 pixels, driven over four SPI wires; the red board with a 14-pin header on one short edge and an SD slot on the back.
1 xabout 9 USD, estimated

Amplifier

Takes digital I2S audio from the microcontroller and drives a 4 to 8 ohm speaker directly at up to about 3 W; no DAC or separate amplifier is needed.
1 xabout 4 USD, estimated

Speaker

A small round loudspeaker for game beeps and tunes; it wires to the amplifier's two speaker terminals, never to a microcontroller pin.
1 xabout 2 USD, estimated

D-pad up

Small push button with a long stem that reaches through an enclosure lid; the four legs are really two electrical connections.
9 xabout 0.10 USD, estimated

Controller

Espressif's own ESP32-S3-DevKitC-1 (v1.0 and v1.1): 25.4 x 62.74 mm, two micro-USB ports, 44 header pins. Most shops sell a different board under the same name, a cheaper dual USB-C clone with fitted headers that is shorter, wider and laid out differently, and this file does not describe it. Read the verify note before ordering a case.
1 xabout 15 USD, estimated

Questions, answered.

Has anyone built this one?

Not yet. The circuit passes a software rule check, the firmware compiles and the case is generated from the part dimensions, but nobody has assembled this exact console or played a game on it. Treat the first build as the test: print the fit test and run the bring-up sketch before you close the case.

How is it different from Cihaz?

Cihaz is the open-source console this one is built from, and the games are theirs. Nokta swaps the thumbstick for a D-pad, adds a battery and charger, replaces jumper leads with soldered wires, leaves out the SD card module that never worked reliably there, and generates the firmware's pins from the wiring.

How long does the battery last?

About six hours by arithmetic: a 7.4 Wh cell against roughly one watt for the controller, the screen and the sound. That is an estimate from typical figures, not a measurement.

Do I need to have soldered before?

It helps. There are 37 wires, and some pads sit 2.54 mm apart. The guide teaches the joint and draws every pad, but this is a second or third project rather than a first.

Is the lithium battery safe?

It is a protected cell on a charger with its own protection, and it plugs in instead of being soldered. Buy the cell named in the parts list, or check that red meets the + mark before plugging in another one. Stay nearby for the first charge, and stop using a cell that is swollen or damaged. If you build this with or for a child, an adult does the battery step.

Can I add my own game?

Yes. Each game is one file with a start function and an update function that runs every frame. Copy the shortest one, change it, and add it to the menu.

Which cables do I need?

Two micro-USB cables at most: any charger cable for the port on the left side, and one that carries data for the top port when you flash new firmware.
